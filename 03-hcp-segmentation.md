# HCP Segmentation and Targeting: Reaching the Prescribers Who Matter

**Deliverable 03 of 07**
**Project:** Padcev First-Line Launch Strategy
**Purpose:** Define the target prescriber universe, establish segmentation criteria, set targeting priorities, and design the engagement strategy for each segment

## Why HCP Segmentation Matters More in Bladder Cancer Than in Most Oncology Indications

Most oncology products are prescribed by a single specialist type: typically a medical oncologist. Bladder cancer is different. The prescribing universe for la/mUC spans multiple specialties, each of which requires a distinct educational approach, promotional message adaptation, and engagement channel.

Getting HCP segmentation right determines how Pfizer allocates its sales force, samples its promotional materials, plans its speaker bureau, and sequences its digital education campaigns. Getting it wrong means over-investing in low-value accounts or under-serving the highest-volume prescribers who drive the majority of treatment decisions.

## Prescriber Segmentation Framework

The standard pharma segmentation framework divides the prescriber universe into tiers based on volume, influence, and accessibility. For Padcev, three dimensions are critical beyond patient volume:

**Dimension 1: Patient volume.** How many la/mUC patients does this physician see in a 12-month period? Physicians with 10 or more la/mUC patients per year represent the highest priority.

**Dimension 2: Prescribing behavior.** Is this physician already using Padcev in 2L+ settings? A physician who has already prescribed Padcev to relapsed patients has seen the product work, understands the adverse event profile, and is a much easier commercial conversation for 1L than a physician who has never used it.

**Dimension 3: Influence and network.** Is this physician a speaker, a clinical trial investigator, a NCCN committee member, or a regional thought leader whose opinion shapes peers? High-influence physicians require a different type of engagement (Medical Affairs, MSL relationships, advisory board participation, speaker bureau) than high-volume-only prescribers.

### The Four Segments

**Segment A: Academic KOLs and High-Volume Specialists (approximately 150 to 200 physicians nationally)**

Profile: Urologic oncologists and GU medical oncologists at academic medical centers. See 20 or more la/mUC patients per year. Have participated in clinical trials (including EV-302 site investigators). Often serve on NCCN guideline committees, present at ASCO/ESMO, and publish in high-impact journals.

Engagement model: Relationship-led. Primary engagement is through Medical Affairs MSLs for scientific exchange and Medical Education programming. Sales representative engagement is appropriate for commercial information only, with clear boundaries between Medical Affairs and commercial. Advisory board participation. National and regional speaker bureau programs.

Commercial priority: These physicians will adopt Padcev for 1L based on the data. The objective is to make them active advocates who communicate the clinical rationale to their peers. This segment is about scientific credibility, not persuasion.

**Segment B: High-Volume Community GU Specialists (approximately 600 to 800 physicians nationally)**

Profile: Medical oncologists and urologic oncologists in large community practices, regional cancer centers, and teaching hospitals without full academic research programs. See 5 to 15 la/mUC patients per year. May have limited trial experience but follow the academic literature and NCCN guidelines.

Engagement model: Sales force-led with MSL support available for clinical questions. Respond to peer-to-peer educational programs featuring Segment A physicians. CME activities and lunch/dinner programs. Digital education through Doceree, Epocrates, and oncology-specific platforms.

Commercial priority: This is where the volume conversion happens. Segment B physicians collectively prescribe more systemic therapy than Segment A. The objective is to equip them with enough clinical confidence to transition their cisplatin-ineligible patients to EV plus Keytruda as first-line treatment immediately.

**Segment C: General Oncologists in Community Settings (approximately 2,000 to 3,000 physicians nationally)**

Profile: General medical oncologists who treat multiple cancer types including bladder cancer but do not have a GU subspecialty focus. May see 1 to 4 la/mUC patients per year. Likely to refer to a specialist for the initial treatment decision but may manage ongoing monitoring and toxicity management.

Engagement model: Digital-first with periodic sales representative contact. Email campaigns, medical congress booth interactions, digital CME. Emphasis on practical clinical tools: dose modification guides, adverse event management cards, infusion administration information.

Commercial priority: Ensure these physicians know when to consider Padcev and, importantly, when to refer a bladder cancer patient to a GU oncology specialist. Minimizing inappropriate referral delays improves time to treatment and patient outcomes.

**Segment D: Urologists Without Oncology Focus (approximately 8,000 to 10,000 physicians nationally)**

Profile: Community urologists who diagnose bladder cancer, perform TURBT, and refer patients for systemic therapy. Occasionally manage systemic therapy in markets without accessible oncologists, but this is becoming less common as targeted therapies require more specialized monitoring.

Engagement model: Limited direct commercial engagement. Disease awareness campaigns. Educational materials focused on when to recognize disease progression to metastatic stage and the importance of timely referral. Patient-facing materials distributed through urology offices.

Commercial priority: These physicians are primarily important as a referral source, not a prescribing target. The objective is to ensure bladder cancer patients are not undertreated or delayed in accessing systemic therapy specialists.

## The Mapping of Influence: Understanding the Decision Ecosystem

Treatment decisions for la/mUC do not happen in isolation. Multiple stakeholders influence what a patient receives:

**The tumor board.** At academic centers and some large community practices, la/mUC cases are discussed in multidisciplinary tumor board meetings. The PMM team should identify which tumor board formats cover GU oncology at target accounts and ensure Padcev's clinical data is accessible in the case discussion context.

**The pharmacist.** Hospital and specialty pharmacists often have formulary approval authority. A drug not on the hospital's formulary requires a P&T committee approval process that can take three to six months. Market access teams must engage hospital pharmacy directors and P&T committees proactively before sales reps begin detailing at those accounts.

**The nurse and infusion center.** Nurses who administer Padcev need training on ADC administration, the pre-medication protocol, adverse event monitoring during infusion, and how to counsel patients on what to watch for at home. Pfizer's field reimbursement managers and specialty nurse educators play a critical role in facilitating this training.

**The patient advocacy community.** Bladder Cancer Advocacy Network (BCAN) is the primary patient advocacy organization. BCAN members are active in sharing treatment information, and BCAN's educational website is a primary resource for newly diagnosed patients researching their options. Pfizer's patient advocacy engagement should include BCAN conference sponsorship, educational grants, and support for BCAN's patient support programs.

## Call Plan and Sales Force Allocation

### Sales Territory Design

For a specialty oncology launch of this type, the sales force structure should align to geographic concentration of target prescribers:

**National account team (approximately 5 to 8 FTEs):** Focused exclusively on the top 15 to 20 academic medical centers. These are institution-level relationships, not individual physician relationships. The national account manager works with P&T committees, oncology pharmacy directors, and tumor board coordinators.

**Specialty sales representatives (approximately 150 to 200 FTEs):** Cover Segments A and B prescribers in defined geographic territories. Call frequency for Segment A: minimum once per quarter, usually supplemented by MSL relationship. Call frequency for Segment B: two to three calls per quarter with increasing frequency around key congress data readouts and practice guideline updates.

**Market access field team (approximately 30 to 50 FTEs):** Support hospital P&T committee submissions, work with specialty pharmacies on prior authorization processes, and help physician offices navigate the Pfizer patient assistance program.

### Prioritization Rule

For the first 90 days of launch, 60% of sales representative activity should focus on Segment B physicians at accounts where Padcev is already on hospital formulary for the 2L indication. These physicians are already familiar with Padcev's administration and toxicity profile. The 1L indication conversation requires one additional piece of information: the EV-302 data. This is the highest-efficiency commercial investment in the launch period.

The remaining 40% of activity should support Segment A KOL relationships (Medical Affairs-led) and accounts where formulary approval is in process.
